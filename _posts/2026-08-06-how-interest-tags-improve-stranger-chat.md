The old random-chat myth was that chaos is the point. Sometimes it is. More often, chaos is just unmatched people talking past each other until someone skips. Interest tags are a mild correction: not a dating app, not a guarantee, just a better first subject than “asl.”

On Omezy you can add interests before you start. Use them as fuel. This piece is how to pick tags that are true, how to combine them without boxing yourself in, and what to do when tags fail — because they will, sometimes, and that is still okay.

## Matching is not a dating filter

A tag is not a promise of romance, friendship, or a five-hour call. It is a shared noun. “Photography” means you both can survive two minutes on cameras or light or the photo you keep meaning to print. It does not mean you are compatible. It does not mean they owe you their weekend.

People get creepy with tags when they treat a match as destiny. “We both like hiking, so you should add me.” No. You both like hiking, so you can talk about a trail, then skip if the rest of the chat is sour. Destiny thinking makes you over-invest in a stranger who clicked the same word.

Tags also are not a purity test. Someone who tagged “music” and then cannot name a band is not a liar. They might have tagged a mood. Ask, don’t prosecute.

Keep the frame adult and light: we might have one thing in common. Let’s see if we can make a conversation out of it. If we cannot, the skip button is working as designed.

## How to pick tags that are true

True means you can talk for five minutes without a search tab. If your tag is “anime” and your last show was eight years ago, you will stall, and stalling on a claimed identity feels like a fake. Pick the thing you actually used this month: cooking, night walks, learning Spanish, cheap travel, bad TV, running, design, cats.

Specific beats vague when you can afford it. “Jazz” is better than “music” if jazz is real for you. “Sourdough” is better than “food.” Specific tags produce fewer matches and better ones. Vague tags produce volume. Decide which problem you want.

Do not tag sexual content if you want a non-sexual chat. You will get what you signaled. Do not tag “language exchange” if you will be annoyed when someone actually practices. Tags are invitations. Honor yours.

Two or three tags is enough. A laundry list looks like you are fishing with every bait in the box. It also makes the first line harder: which tag do you even mention?

Revisit tags when your life changes. The tag set you used in winter may be wrong in summer. Treat them as seasonal, not as a brand.

## Combining tags without boxing yourself in

Two tags create a Venn diagram. “Language learning” plus “travel” is a natural pair. “Gaming” plus “night owl” tells a story. “Photography” plus “hiking” is a weekend. Combinations give you an opener: “You’re the travel-and-language person. Are you packing, studying, or procrastinating both?”

Avoid combinations that only make sense as a trap. If your tags are all appearance-adjacent and your first line is about their body, the tags were never interests. They were cover.

Leave one tag broad if you want air. A tight pair plus one loose tag (“music”) keeps you from only matching clones of yourself. Random chat still needs a little randomness. Tags should tilt the odds, not delete surprise.

If you are in a low-energy mood, pick tags that support small talk: food, weather-of-the-week, shows. If you want a slightly real chat, pick tags that admit interior life: books, language, walking, making things. Mood and tags should match, or you will resent the people who took you at your word.

## When tags fail

They fail when the other person used the tag as bait. They fail when the match is technically shared but the vibe is not. They fail when the system pairs you with someone who tagged the same word in a different dialect of meaning — your “running” is marathons, theirs is “I ran once.”

When that happens, do not lecture. You can try one clarifying question: “When you say running, is that a real hobby or a hopeful tag?” If they laugh and admit hopeful, you can still talk. If they get hostile, skip.

Tags also fail when you hide behind them. A five-minute encyclopedia dump about a hobby is not a conversation. Ask them where they are in the hobby: beginner, obsessed, rusty. Meet them there.

If three tagged matches in a row are duds, clear the tags and go fully random for a bit, or stop. The tool is not broken just because Tuesday is a dry well. Random chat has weather.

## Using tags as conversation fuel

The first mention should be a fork, not a quiz. Quiz: “Name three films.” Fork: “Comfort rewatch or something new this week?” Quizzes have wrong answers. Forks have preferences.

Second mention should include you. “I tagged cooking because I keep making the same soup. I’m not proud.” Now they can one-up you, comfort you, or share a recipe. All three are chat.

Third mention should leave the tag if the chat has another thread. The goal is to become two people talking, not two profiles comparing keywords. If you never leave the tag, the conversation feels like a forum thread. If you never use the tag, you wasted the match.

Close tagged chats the same way you close untagged ones: cleanly. “I’m gonna go — thanks for the trail rec.” You do not owe a social media swap. If you both want to continue, that is a later decision on a later night.

Interest tags will not make strangers kind. They will not make you fluent. They will give you a first sentence that is about a thing instead of a body. That is a low, decent bar, and it is why starting on Omezy with a couple of honest tags is usually a better night than starting with nothing but hope.
